Euclidean geometry is a mathematical system attributed to the ancient Greek mathematician Euclid, which he described in his textbook "Elements" around 300 BCE. It is the study of plane and solid figures based on axioms and theorems derived from them. Here are the key components of Euclidean geometry:

1. **Axioms/Postulates**: Euclidean geometry is based on five fundamental axioms or postulates:
   - A straight line segment can be drawn joining any two points.
   - Any straight line segment can be extended indefinitely in a straight line.
   - Given any straight line segment, a circle can be drawn having the segment as radius and one endpoint as the center.
   - All right angles are congruent.
   - If two lines are drawn which intersect a third in such a way that the sum of the inner angles on one side is less than two right angles, then the two lines inevitably must intersect each other on that side if extended far enough (the parallel postulate).

2. **Basic Concepts**:
   - **Points**: Locations in space with no dimensions (length, width, height).
   - **Lines**: Infinitely long and thin continuous extent of points.
   - **Planes**: Flat, two-dimensional surfaces extending infinitely in all directions.
   - **Angles**: Formed by two rays (sides of the angle) sharing a common endpoint (vertex).

3. **Figures and Shapes**:
   - **Triangles**: Three-sided polygons.
   - **Quadrilaterals**: Four-sided polygons, including special types like squares, rectangles, and parallelograms.
   - **Circles**: Sets of all points in a plane at a given distance (radius) from a given point (center).

4. **Theorems and Proofs**: In Euclidean geometry, properties of figures are derived and proven based on the axioms. Famous theorems include:
   - Pythagorean Theorem: In a right-angled triangle, the square of the length of the hypotenuse is equal to the sum of the squares of the lengths of the other two sides.
   - The sum of the angles in a triangle is always 180 degrees.

Euclidean geometry is the foundation of many areas of mathematics and science. It is also known as "plane geometry" when it deals with two-dimensional figures, and "solid geometry" when it deals with three-dimensional figures.

A "nuisance" generally refers to something or someone that causes inconvenience, annoyance, or discomfort. The term can be applied in various contexts, including everyday language, law, and environmental studies.

### General Definition
In everyday usage, a nuisance is any person, thing, or circumstance causing inconvenience or annoyance. Examples include:
- A noisy neighbor
- An unpleasant smell
- Persistent unwanted solicitations

### Legal Definition
In legal terms, a nuisance is an act, condition, or thing that interferes with the use or enjoyment of property. There are two main types:
1. **Public Nuisance**: Affects the community or public at large. Examples include:
   - Pollution of air or water
   - Obstruction of public highways
   - Operation of a house of prostitution

2. **Private Nuisance**: Affects a specific individual or a small group of individuals. Examples include:
   - Loud noises from a neighbor's property
   - Emission of smoke or odors from a neighboring property
   - Overhanging branches or roots from a neighbor’s tree

### Environmental Studies
In environmental contexts, a nuisance might refer to any factor that disturbs the natural environment or ecosystem, such as:
- Invasive species disrupting local habitats
- Industrial pollution affecting air and water quality
- Noise pollution affecting wildlife

### Examples in Daily Life
- A barking dog disturbing the peace of the neighborhood
- Construction work causing noise and dust
- A malfunctioning streetlight creating light pollution

In all contexts, addressing nuisances typically involves finding a balance between the rights and responsibilities of individuals and the community, often requiring mediation, regulation, or legal action to resolve conflicts and mitigate the negative impacts.
