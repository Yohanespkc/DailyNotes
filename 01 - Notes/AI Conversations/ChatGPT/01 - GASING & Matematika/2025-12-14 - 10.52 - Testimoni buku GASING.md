## Discussion: Why Papua Shows the Largest Gains Under the GASING Method

One of the most striking findings in the nationwide implementation of the GASING method is that the **largest relative learning gains consistently occur in Papua**, a region historically characterized by very low baseline numeracy. Across multiple Papuan provinces—Papua, Papua Tengah, Papua Pegunungan, Papua Barat, and Papua Barat Daya—student performance improvements frequently exceed 10× and, in several districts, reach extreme levels of 20–30× within short training periods. At first glance, such results may appear anomalous; however, closer analysis reveals that these outcomes are theoretically coherent and pedagogically explainable.

### 1. Extremely Low Baseline and the “Unlocked Potential” Effect

Papua’s historically low numeracy outcomes are well documented and are primarily attributed to structural factors rather than cognitive limitations: geographic isolation, limited instructional resources, shortages of trained mathematics teachers, and long-standing reliance on rote, abstract instruction disconnected from students’ lived experiences. Consequently, baseline numeracy scores in many Papuan districts are extremely low. In such contexts, even moderate absolute improvements translate into very large relative gains.

However, the magnitude of the observed improvements—often exceeding 15× or even 30×—cannot be explained by a statistical floor effect alone. Instead, the data suggest an **“unlocked potential” phenomenon**, whereby students who had never successfully crossed fundamental conceptual thresholds in arithmetic rapidly do so once instruction becomes concrete, structured, and emotionally safe. GASING’s emphasis on critical learning points (rather than linear syllabus coverage) appears particularly effective in such environments, enabling students to overcome long-standing conceptual bottlenecks in a short time span fileciteturn1file0.

### 2. Alignment with Oral, Embodied, and Communal Learning Cultures

A second explanatory factor lies in the strong alignment between GASING pedagogy and local learning cultures in Papua. GASING relies heavily on **oral explanation, rhythmic repetition, movement, collective response, and embodied cognition**—features that resonate strongly with Papuan communal traditions. Learning activities involving chanting, synchronized counting, clapping, singing, and group problem-solving mirror existing cultural practices, reducing cognitive and affective resistance to formal schooling.

In contrast to conventional textbook-centered instruction, which often alienates learners in remote contexts, GASING situates mathematics as a shared, lived activity. This cultural congruence lowers psychological barriers to participation and accelerates conceptual uptake, particularly among students who may struggle with written abstraction but excel in oral and kinesthetic modes of learning.

### 3. Reduction of Math Anxiety in High-Fear Contexts

Neuroscience research has shown that mathematics anxiety activates brain regions associated with threat and pain, impairing working memory and executive function. This effect is magnified in contexts where students have experienced repeated failure or punitive instructional practices. In Papua, both students and teachers frequently report long-standing fear of mathematics, sometimes spanning generations.

GASING directly targets this affective barrier through joyful, non-punitive instruction and rapid early success. The data show that once fear is removed, learning accelerates dramatically. This helps explain why Papua—despite starting from the lowest baseline—exhibits the **largest relative gains**: the affective release is proportionally greater. When anxiety is reduced, cognitive resources become immediately available for learning, producing rapid improvement in arithmetic performance fileciteturn1file2.

### 4. Teacher Transformation as a Multiplicative Factor

Another critical factor is the transformation of teacher competence and confidence. In many Papuan districts, participating teachers initially reported limited mastery of basic arithmetic. GASING training does not assume prior mathematical expertise; instead, it rebuilds teachers’ understanding from first principles using the same concrete–abstract–mental progression applied to students.

Empirical data show that teachers in Papua typically improve their own numeracy by approximately 2–4× following GASING training. This improvement is pedagogically significant: as teachers’ confidence increases, their instructional clarity, emotional regulation, and willingness to engage students also improve. In classrooms where teachers transition from anxious or authoritarian styles to confident and compassionate facilitation, student learning gains are amplified. This teacher-level transformation functions as a **multiplicative effect**, partially explaining why student gains in Papua exceed those observed in regions with stronger initial teacher preparation fileciteturn1file0.

### 5. Concentrated, Intensive Intervention Design

Finally, the structure of GASING implementation in Papua contributes to its effectiveness. Most programs are delivered as **intensive, immersive interventions** over approximately two weeks, with sustained daily practice and minimal instructional fragmentation. In contexts where students previously received irregular or low-density mathematics instruction, this concentrated exposure produces rapid consolidation of skills.

The combination of intensive time-on-task, immediate feedback, emotional safety, and culturally aligned pedagogy creates optimal conditions for accelerated learning. Importantly, similar intervention designs in other regions do not produce gains of the same magnitude unless baseline conditions are comparably constrained, reinforcing the conclusion that Papua’s exceptional outcomes are a product of contextual fit rather than anomaly.

### Implications

Taken together, these findings suggest that Papua does not represent an outlier in terms of learner capability, but rather an **extreme case that reveals the underlying mechanics of effective numeracy instruction**. The very regions once labeled as “low-performing” demonstrate the greatest capacity for rapid improvement when instructional barriers are removed. This challenges deficit-based narratives about students in remote or disadvantaged areas and supports the core GASING premise: numeracy failure is primarily pedagogical, not cognitive.

From a policy perspective, the Papuan results indicate that targeted, compassion-based, methodologically precise interventions can generate disproportionately large returns in high-need contexts. Rather than requiring years of incremental reform, foundational numeracy gaps can be closed rapidly when teaching methods align with how children learn—cognitively, emotionally, and culturally.
