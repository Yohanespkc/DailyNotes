**Supplementary Note 9.7: Why Creativity Emerges, Not Is Taught**

The eight activities described in Table 9.7 share a common design principle: none of them asks students to "be creative." What each activity does instead is create a condition in which something new must be produced because nothing already known is sufficient.

In the **Logical-Mathematical** activity, students are not told where a pattern ends. After discovering that multiples of 4 always increase by 4, a student is asked: what happens if we extend this to numbers the class has never worked with before, such as 4 × 23 or 4 × 47? There is no memorised answer to retrieve. The only way forward is to extend the structure into unknown territory. Nowhere is this more vivid than in what happens when students search for patterns in the 9-times table. Given the sequence 9, 18, 27, 36, 45, every student finds a different route. Some notice that the two digits of each product always sum to 9. Others observe that the tens digit is always one less than the multiplier. Still others prefer the kinesthetic shortcut of bending fingers in sequence. No single method is taught; no single method is declared correct. The diversity of strategies that emerges is not a problem to be managed. It is the very essence of mathematical creativity, proof that when a structure is deep enough, it can be entered from many directions at once.

In the **Spatial-Visual** activity, students learn to draw multiplication as a picture: 6 × 4 is represented as 6 boxes, each containing 4 marbles. The image makes the meaning of multiplication concrete and visible. Creativity emerges when the same student is then challenged to draw 6 × 24. Two paths open immediately. The student could draw 6 boxes each containing 24 marbles, which is faithful but unwieldy. Or the student could invent a new visual language: 6 boxes each containing 2 large marbles and 4 small marbles, where one large marble represents ten small ones. This second path is not taught. It is discovered under pressure. The student who chooses it has done something genuinely creative: they have extended a known visual form into new territory by inventing a representational convention that did not previously exist for them. The drawing is no longer just a picture of multiplication. It is a picture of place value, arrived at through the student's own reasoning.

In the **Intrapersonal** activity, a student who has confidently established that 3 × 2 = 6 is asked a quiet but unsettling question: why does 30 × 2 = 60? And why, stranger still, does 3 × 20 give exactly the same result? No explanation is offered. The student is asked to sit with the question and trace their own thinking. The first instinct is often to say "because you add a zero," but the GASING visual immediately challenges that shortcut: is 30 × 2 really 30 boxes each containing 2 marbles? And if so, why is that the same as 3 boxes each containing 20 marbles, where each marble is a large marble representing ten? The student who wrestles with this question long enough begins to see something that was never stated: that multiplication is about the total quantity, and that the same total can be arranged in fundamentally different ways without changing its value. That insight is not delivered by a teacher. It surfaces from within, through the student's own sustained attention to a question they themselves could not yet answer. Creativity emerges at precisely that moment: when private reasoning produces an understanding that is genuinely the student's own.

In the **Interpersonal** activity, six students are invited to the front of the room. They are asked to invent two funny movements, each their own, with no coordination between them. One student might decide that their first movement is a head-bobbing bird imitation and their second is a jumping monkey. Another student makes entirely different choices. No one knows what anyone else has chosen. When the leader gives the signal, all six perform simultaneously with eyes closed, each expressing their own private decision freely and joyfully. Then someone counts: four students are doing the bird movement, two are doing the monkey. The class records this as 6 = 4 + 2. The activity repeats, and the random distribution of free choices produces 6 = 5 + 1, then 6 = 3 + 3. Every decomposition of the number emerges naturally, not from a teacher writing combinations on a board, but from the genuine unpredictability of six individuals making independent creative choices within a shared structure. Creativity here is not a metaphor. Each student's choice of movement is a small act of personal expression. The mathematics is the residue of that expression, what remains when six individual creativities meet and are counted. No single student produced the decomposition. No teacher planned which split would appear. The group created it, in the fullest sense of the word, by being freely and joyfully themselves.

In the **Bodily-Kinaesthetic** activity, numbers 1 through 9 are written on the floor in a large arrangement, together with addition and multiplication signs. One player steps into the space and poses: right hand touching 3, one hand on the multiplication sign, left foot on 2, right foot on 4. The opponent reads the body as a mathematical sentence, 3 × 24, and must immediately respond by posing the answer with their own body, left hand on 7, right hand or foot on 2, with remaining limbs touching the operator symbols required to complete the expression. Then they switch roles. Every member of a group of five takes a turn, and the group's total time is measured. The group that completes all rounds fastest and most accurately wins. What makes this activity a genuine site of creativity is that the body is not illustrating mathematics from the outside. The body is the notation. To pose a question, a player must first decide which expression to encode, then solve the spatial puzzle of how to distribute four limbs across the floor to make it readable. To pose an answer, a player must decompose a number on the spot, decide how many digits to show and in what arrangement, and then make that decision legible through physical placement alone. Every pose is an original construction. Creativity emerges because the medium demands it: a body that hesitates or defaults to habit will be read incorrectly, and the team will lose time. Invention, in this game, is not optional.

In the **Linguistic-Verbal** activity, a student conducting microteaching is given the problem 12 − 7 and asked to explain it to their peers using only their own words, with no formula, no written working, and no memorised script. The student must construct a verbal sequence that makes the invisible process of borrowing audible and followable. One student might say it this way: twelve minus seven, the units cannot be subtracted because two is smaller than seven, so we break one ten into ten units, now we have ten units minus seven which gives three units, but we still had two units from before, so the answer is three plus two, which is five. Every word in that sequence is a decision. The student must choose when to introduce the idea of breaking a ten, how to name the units that remain, and how to bring the two parts of the calculation back together at the end in a way that a classmate can follow without confusion. No two students construct this explanation in exactly the same way. Each formulation is an original verbal creation, a small piece of mathematical writing composed in real time under the pressure of being understood. Creativity emerges from the constraint itself: because the student cannot borrow someone else's words, they must find their own, and in finding them, they understand the mathematics more deeply than any written exercise would require.

In the **Musical-Rhythmic** activity, participants in a two-week GASING training are given a challenge that cannot be completed during training hours. The only time available is the weekend break between the two weeks of training. There is no studio, no music teacher, no script. There is only a mathematical idea, a cultural tradition, a small group of people who may never have composed a song together before, and two days. What happens in those two days is not easily explained by any theory of instruction. Groups who have never thought of themselves as musicians find themselves arguing about melody, negotiating lyrics, and discovering that the syllable count of *tujuh tambah tiga* fits one rhythm but not another. Someone picks up a *tifa* or a *kolintang* or a *gamelan* fragment and tries it against the words. Someone else suggests a call-and-response structure. A line is rewritten four times until it lands correctly on the beat. By Sunday evening, something exists that did not exist on Friday: a song, a video, a performance, and a group of people who made it together under conditions that offered no easy exit. Creativity emerges under pressure precisely because pressure removes the option of waiting for a better idea. The deadline is real, the audience is real, and the mathematics must be correct. When those three conditions meet, invention is not a choice. It is the only way through.

In the **Naturalist** activity, students are asked to mentally calculate 51², 52², 53², and 54² using the GASING method, then write out the results: 2601, 2704, 2809, 2916. The calculation itself is not the challenge. The challenge comes immediately after, when the teacher says nothing and simply waits. Students are asked to look at what they have written and find whatever pattern is hiding there. The last two digits are 01, 04, 09, 16: the squares of 1, 2, 3, and 4, the units digits of the original numbers. The group extends the pattern to 55² through 59² and confirms it holds. Then comes the harder question: what about 41² through 49²? Students calculate and write: 1681, 1764, 1849, 1936, 2025, 2116, 2209, 2304, 2401. The last two digits run in reverse: 81, 64, 49, 36, 25, 16, 09, 04, 01. These are squares, but they are not the squares of the units digits. They are the squares of 9, 8, 7, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1. The student who has learned pasangan sepuluh deeply feels the recognition before they can name it: the last two digits of 41² are governed not by 1, but by the pasangan of 1, which is 9. The series that began as a curiosity about squares turns out to carry the fingerprint of pasangan sepuluh, a concept the students learned when they were just beginning to count. The teacher who witnessed this moment described what happened when the connection became visible: the room changed. Students who had been quietly calculating suddenly sat up. The joy was not the joy of getting a right answer. It was the joy of seeing something that had been invisible become suddenly, irreversibly visible. Creativity in the naturalist mode emerges exactly here: not from inventing something new, but from perceiving a connection that was always present but never seen.

In each case, the activity removes the ready-made answer. There is no template to follow, no correct form to reproduce, no authority to consult. The student must produce something that did not exist before the activity began. That condition, the removal of the pre-given, is what creativity requires. GASING does not teach creativity. It builds the conditions in which creativity has no choice but to appear.

**Creativity Beyond the Classroom: Field Evidence from GASING Training**

The creativity that GASING cultivates does not remain inside the mathematics lesson. Three incidents from the field, each unremarkable on the surface, each quietly extraordinary, illustrate what becomes possible when a culture of creative problem-solving takes root.

In Hulu Sungai Tengah, a team of teachers and students invented an entirely original game during training. Answers to multi-digit addition problems were written on cards and hung from trees across the school grounds. A mixed team had to locate and retrieve the correct cards, with blindfolded students at the front guided by teammates behind them. The game integrated mathematics, physical movement, teamwork, and joy in a single activity that no curriculum had prescribed. It was born from a simple challenge issued during teacher training: invent something. The teachers accepted the challenge and produced something that had never existed before.

*Figure 9.11. "Answer Hunt" game, Hulu Sungai Tengah. Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rqpmTt9C2Ek*

In Sangihe Regency, North Sulawesi, the electricity cut out mid-training. Neither the trainers nor the participants stopped. Together they pushed a car into the training room and used its battery to power the speaker and microphone. The training continued without interruption. What is notable here is not the solution itself, which was ingenious, but the speed and unanimity with which it was reached. No one waited to be told what to do. The creative response was collective and immediate, the natural behaviour of a group that had already internalised the habit of finding a way forward.

*Figure 9.12. Trainers and teachers pushing a car into the training room, Sangihe. Source: https://youtu.be/oKt1GkstENI*

In Bojonegoro Regency, East Java, evening training was interrupted by a power cut that plunged the room into complete darkness. The reasonable response would have been to end the session and send everyone home. Instead, dozens of teachers raised their mobile phones into the air, torches blazing, and held them there until the room was lit. The training continued. They refused to leave because, for many of them, it was the first time learning mathematics had felt genuinely enjoyable. They would not allow the darkness to take that from them.

*Figure 9.13. Teachers raising phones as torches, Bojonegoro, East Java. Source: https://youtu.be/3qPoXMPVkYA*

These three incidents share a common structure: an obstacle appears, no solution is provided, and the group produces one. That structure is identical to the structure of every creative moment described in this chapter. The difference is only the scale and the setting. The student who invents a new way to represent 6 × 24, and the teacher who pushes a car into a training room so the learning can continue, are doing the same thing. They are refusing to stop at the edge of what they already know.

Communication is the capacity to transfer information or ideas clearly, effectively, and accessibly, both orally and in writing. The P21 framework (Battelle for Kids, 2019) defines it as the ability to articulate thoughts and ideas clearly, listen effectively to decode meaning, use communication for a range of purposes, and engage effectively in diverse environments.

**Supplementary Note 9.X: Why Communication Emerges, Not Is Taught**

The eight activities described in Table 9.X share a common design principle: none of them instructs students to "communicate well." What each activity does instead is create a situation in which communication becomes necessary, because the task cannot be completed through private understanding alone.

In the **Logical-Mathematical** activity, a student who can calculate 9 × 7 correctly is asked why the answer follows the same structure as 9 × 3. The calculation requires no words. The explanation requires many. The student must sequence their reasoning, decide which step comes first, and find language for a relationship that previously existed only as a felt pattern. Communication emerges from the gap between knowing and saying: the student who can calculate but cannot explain has discovered, through the attempt to speak, exactly where their understanding ends.

In the **Spatial-Visual** activity, a student who has drawn the marble-box representation of 6 × 24, with large marbles representing tens and small marbles representing units, is asked to describe the drawing to a partner who cannot see it. Every detail that seemed obvious in the picture must now be encoded in words: how many boxes, how many marbles of each kind, and what the size difference means. Communication emerges from the translation between modes: what the eye grasps instantly, the voice must reconstruct piece by piece, and the discipline of that reconstruction reveals which parts of the visual understanding were clear and which were assumed without being examined.

In the **Intrapersonal** activity, a student who has made an error is asked not to correct it but to write out, in complete sentences, every step they took from the beginning of the problem. The writing is addressed to themselves, but the standard imposed is that of a reader who knows nothing. A student working through 30 × 2 who wrote "because you add a zero" must now ask: is that an explanation, or merely a description? Does it say *why* the zero appears, or only *that* it appears? Communication emerges from self-address: the attempt to write clearly to oneself exposes the difference between familiarity and understanding.

In the **Interpersonal** activity, two students solve the same problem independently and reach different answers. They are not permitted to ask a teacher. Each must explain their full process to the other, and the explanation must be specific enough that the listener can identify exactly where the two paths diverged. Communication emerges from the pressure of a genuine disagreement: vague explanations do not resolve disputes, and the only path to resolution is a level of precision that casual conversation never demands. The conversation ends not when one student persuades the other, but when both can articulate, in words, exactly why one path was correct and the other was not.

In the **Bodily-Kinaesthetic** activity, a student who has posed 3 × 24 with their body must wait while their opponent reads the pose and then names it aloud: "three times twenty-four." If the reading is wrong, the poser must explain, without moving, what the pose was intended to mean and how it should have been read. The error becomes a communication problem: not a failure of mathematics but a failure of encoding or decoding. Communication emerges from the mismatch between what was expressed and what was understood, and the repair of that mismatch requires both parties to make their interpretive assumptions explicit.

In the **Linguistic-Verbal** activity, a student explaining 12 − 7 in microteaching is not permitted to write on the board, use symbols, or recite a memorised procedure. The explanation must stand entirely on its own as spoken language. When a peer raises their hand and says "I don't understand the part about breaking the ten," the student cannot repeat the same words more slowly. They must find different words, a different sequence, a different entry point into the same idea. Communication emerges from the requirement to be genuinely understood: not to have spoken, but to have been heard.

In the **Musical-Rhythmic** activity, a group that has composed a pasangan sepuluh song using a local melody is asked, at the closing ceremony, to explain their creative process to the audience before performing. They must say which mathematical concept the song encodes, why they chose the melody they chose, and what they had to change when the syllables of the mathematics did not fit the rhythm they intended. **Collaboration**

Collaboration is the ability to work effectively with others, respect differing viewpoints, and achieve shared goals. The P21 framework (Battelle for Kids, 2019) defines it as the capacity to work effectively and respectfully in diverse teams, demonstrate flexibility in reaching compromise to achieve shared objectives, and share joint responsibility for team outcomes.

Most learning environments attempt to create these two conditions by instruction: students are told to cooperate, assigned roles, and reminded to include everyone. It places them in situations where collaboration is the only viable strategy, because the task is genuinely impossible to complete alone, and because helping another person succeed turns out to be the most direct route to one's own understanding.

**Definition and Background**

Computational logic is the capacity to decompose complex problems into algorithmic steps, recognise systematic patterns, and design procedures that can be reliably replicated. Jeannette Wing (2006), in her influential paper on computational thinking, identifies four principal components: decomposition, breaking complex problems into smaller manageable parts; pattern recognition, identifying recurring regularities; abstraction, focusing on the essence of a problem while setting aside irrelevant detail; and algorithm design, constructing systematic steps toward a replicable solution.

These four components are the foundation of computer programming. They are also, as it turns out, the foundation of everything a GASING student does every time they solve a subtraction, a multiplication, or a division problem. The student does not know this. That is precisely the point.

---

**The Hidden Algorithm**

Consider 64 − 7. A student who has never encountered computational thinking solves this problem the GASING way: check whether the units digit can be subtracted directly; if not, decompose one ten into ten units; subtract from the expanded units; recombine with the remaining tens; state the result.

Read that sequence again, slowly. There is a condition check. There is a branch decision. There is a decomposition step. There is a recombination step. There is an output.

This is not arithmetic. This is a program. And GASING children run this program, in its various forms across all four BAKALKUBAGI operations, dozens of times every day, without ever being told that what they are practising has a name in computer science.
